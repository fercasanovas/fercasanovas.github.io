We Checked 50 B2B SaaS Sites for AI Crawler Access. Here Is What We Found

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

Two-thirds publish an llms.txt — a convention no AI platform has committed to reading. Only 17% name a single AI crawler in robots.txt, the file that actually governs access. Companies are adopting the new thing faster than they are managing the established one.

What Was Measured, and How

Fifty widely-known B2B SaaS and marketing companies, measured on 21 September 2026. These are companies whose buyers plausibly ask an AI assistant for recommendations.

Only public files were read:

  • robots.txt — present, parseable, and what it says about eight named AI crawlers.
  • llms.txt — present and served as real text, not a soft 404 dressed as one.
  • Homepage JSON-LD — what structured data types appear.

Nothing behind a login. Nothing republished. No company is named as failing anything — only aggregate counts appear below.

The honest limits of this sample

  • Fifty is a sample, not a census. These are large, well-resourced companies. A local dentist's site will look nothing like this.
  • Three of the fifty had no usable robots.txt, so crawler percentages below are of 47.
  • A robots.txt rule is a request, not a wall. It records intent, not enforcement.
  • This measures readability, not citation. Whether these companies are actually named in AI answers is a separate question that this data does not touch.

How Many Sites Block AI Crawlers?

Almost none. Two of 47.

The blocking panic did not happen. The story of publishers shutting out AI crawlers is real for news media. Among B2B SaaS companies selling to buyers who research with AI assistants, it is not happening.

But 83% silence is not a decision. It is the absence of one. Those sites are readable by default, which is probably what they want — they just have not said so.

What that means for an audit

A client whose robots.txt says nothing about AI crawlers is in the majority. That is worth telling them plainly, because it reframes the finding.

The question is not "why are we blocking?" It is "have we ever decided?"

Why Is llms.txt Adoption So High?

Thirty-three of fifty publish one. That is 66% — roughly four times the rate at which the same companies explicitly manage AI crawler access in robots.txt.

Each was fetched and checked to be real text, because a site returning its homepage for any unknown path would otherwise be counted as publishing one. All were genuine.

This is the finding worth sitting with. llms.txt is a proposed convention. No standards body has ratified it.

No major platform has published a commitment to reading it. It is not access control, and nothing is obliged to respect it.

It is being adopted anyway, at scale, by companies with real engineering budgets.

Two readings, and the honest answer is probably both

  • Cheap insurance. An hour of work, no known downside, and a position if adoption arrives. That is the same reasoning this site's own page on llms.txt reaches.
  • Visible effort. Publishing one is legible to a board in a way that editing robots.txt is not.

Neither reading makes it effective. Adoption is not evidence that anything reads the file. It is evidence that the cost is low.

Under half declare who they are in machine-readable form. For companies this size that is lower than expected. Entity clarity is also one of the few things a consultant can fix quickly and verifiably, whatever it turns out to be worth for citation.

What a Consultant Can Do With This

  • Check the client's robots.txt first. Most say nothing, and "nothing" is a finding worth reporting as a decision not yet taken.
  • Do not lead with llms.txt. Two-thirds of large SaaS companies publish one and no platform has committed to reading it. It is cheap, so it belongs at the bottom of a fix list, not the top.
  • Check Organization schema. Half of these companies are missing it, so a smaller client very likely is too.
  • Do not confuse readable with cited. Everything here measures whether a site can be read. Whether it is named in an answer is a different measurement entirely, and it is checked by asking the platforms directly.

Reproducing This

The script is public and the domain list is in the repository. Anyone can re-run it and get their own numbers, which is the point — a finding nobody can reproduce is a claim, not a result.

Re-running it in six months would be more interesting than running it once. The llms.txt number in particular is a bet on a convention, and bets resolve.
