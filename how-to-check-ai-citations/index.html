How Do You Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited in AI Search?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

Sign out, open a private window, and ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google the same realistic buyer question about the client's category — not its name. Note whether the client appears, where, and how it's described. Check again a day or two later: a single check tells you what happened once, not what's true.

What's the Fastest Way to Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited?

The fastest honest version of this check starts with a private browser window, not the one open all day.

Sign out of every account you can. A signed-in ChatGPT or Gemini session carries your own search and chat history behind it, which means the answer you get back is shaped for you specifically, not representative of what a stranger typing the same question would see. Ask a category question, not a name question — "who handles [the service the client sells] for [a type of buyer]" rather than "tell me about [client name]." The second question tells you almost nothing, because most engines will happily discuss a brand you've just named regardless of whether they'd have surfaced it unprompted.

What Questions Should You Actually Ask to Check AI Visibility?

The question matters more than the platform, and most manual checks get the question wrong before a browser tab even opens.

A single question isn't enough, because different buyer situations surface different answers from the same engine. At minimum, check a version of each of these shapes: a buyer who hasn't named any vendor yet and is asking what the options even are; a buyer who already knows the client's name and is asking how it compares to something specific; a buyer with a real constraint — a budget, a timeline, a location — asking who they should actually pick; and a buyer who named a competitor and is asking what else is out there. A client can look strong on one of these and be effectively invisible on another, and a single "how do we show up in ChatGPT" question only ever tests one of the four.

A full audit runs a meaningfully larger version of this same idea — a fixed set of roughly twelve questions across these four shapes, checked on all four platforms, producing around 48 individually tracked results — precisely because a handful of manual checks is a sanity check, not a measurement a client can act on with confidence.

Why Does It Matter Whether You're Signed In When You Check?

Personalization is real, and it's the single most common reason a quick manual check gets contradicted a week later.

An account with search or chat history behind it gets an answer shaped partly by that history, which is useful to the account holder and actively misleading to anyone trying to see what a stranger sees. Gemini in particular is hard to check fully signed out; where sign-in is unavoidable, the closest approximation is a fresh account with personalization and history-saving turned off, treated as a known limitation rather than ignored.

Why Check More Than Once Before Drawing a Conclusion?

These systems are not deterministic, and treating one answer as the truth is the single most common mistake in this kind of check.

The same question, asked again a day or two later, can come back with a different set of brands, different wording, or a different tone entirely — a model update, an index refresh, or simple variance in the system can move the result with nothing on the client's own site changing at all. A result that holds steady across two separate days is worth more confidence than one that was only ever checked once; a result that flips between two checks is itself the finding — it means the client's position in that answer is unsettled, not that either check was wrong.

What Should You Write Down When You Check?

Recording the answer before forming an opinion about it is the habit that separates a useful check from a wasted one.

For each check, note: the exact question asked, the platform, the date, whether the client was named and where in the answer (first, buried in a list, or not at all), whether the description was accurate, and which other brands came up alongside it. Paste the actual response text somewhere you control rather than relying on memory or a screenshot you'll lose track of — a share link from the platform itself can expire or get deleted before anyone reads it again, and it's not a substitute for keeping the raw text.

What a Manual Citation Check Can't Tell You

A fifteen-minute manual check like this is a real, useful signal, and it is not the same thing as a full measurement — the gap between the two is worth naming plainly rather than glossed over.

It can't tell you whether a result is genuinely stable from only two checks; that's still a small sample of a system that changes without warning. It can't tell you why a citation is missing — that takes checking the client's own site directly for whether the relevant crawlers can even read it, a different kind of check entirely. And it can't be turned into a defensible score for a client without a documented, repeatable method behind it: a number a client could ask you to reproduce and get a materially different answer back is a liability in a delivery call, not an asset.

No specific citation, ranking, or traffic outcome is guaranteed for any brand; answer engines select their sources using undisclosed criteria that change without notice.
