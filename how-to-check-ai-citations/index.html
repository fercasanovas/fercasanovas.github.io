How Do You Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited in AI Search?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

Sign out, open a private window, and ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google the same realistic buyer question about the client's category — not its name. Note whether the client appears, where, and how it's described. Check again a day or two later. A single check tells you what happened once, not what's true.

What's the Fastest Way to Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited?

The fastest honest version of this check starts with a private browser window, not the one open all day.

Sign out of every account you can. A signed-in ChatGPT or Gemini session carries your own search and chat history behind it. That means the answer you get back is shaped for you specifically, not for a stranger asking the same question.

Ask a category question, not a name question. Try "who handles this for a buyer like me" rather than "tell me about this client."

The name question tells you almost nothing. Most engines will happily discuss a brand you've just named, regardless of whether they'd have surfaced it unprompted.

What Questions Should You Actually Ask to Check AI Visibility?

The question matters more than the platform. Most manual checks get the question wrong before a browser tab even opens.

A single question isn't enough, because different buyer situations surface different answers from the same engine. At minimum, check four shapes.

A buyer who hasn't named any vendor yet is asking what the options even are. A buyer who already knows the client's name is asking how it compares to something specific. A buyer with a real constraint — a budget, a timeline, a location — is asking who they should actually pick. A buyer who named a competitor is asking what else is out there.

A client can look strong on one of these and be effectively invisible on another. A single "how do we show up in ChatGPT" question only ever tests one of the four.

A full audit runs a meaningfully larger version of this same idea. It checks a fixed set of roughly twelve questions across these four shapes, on all four platforms, producing around 48 individually tracked results. A handful of manual checks is a sanity check, not a measurement a client can act on with confidence.

Why Does It Matter Whether You're Signed In When You Check?

Personalization is real. It's the single most common reason a quick manual check gets contradicted a week later.

An account with search or chat history behind it gets an answer shaped partly by that history. That's useful to the account holder and actively misleading to anyone trying to see what a stranger sees. Gemini in particular is hard to check fully signed out. Where sign-in is unavoidable, the closest approximation is a fresh account with personalization and history-saving turned off. Treat that as a known limitation, not something to ignore.

Why Check More Than Once Before Drawing a Conclusion?

These systems are not deterministic. Treating one answer as the truth is the single most common mistake in this kind of check.

The same question, asked on two different days, can come back with a different set of brands, different wording, or a different tone entirely. A model update, an index refresh, or simple variance in the system can move the result — with nothing on the client's own site changing at all.

A result that holds steady across two separate days is worth more confidence than one that was only ever checked once. A result that flips between two checks is itself the finding. It means the client's position in that answer is unsettled, not that either check was wrong.

What Should You Write Down When You Check?

Recording the answer before forming an opinion about it is the habit that separates a useful check from a wasted one.

For each check, note the exact question asked, the platform, and the date. Note whether the client was named, and where — first, buried in a list, or not at all. Note whether the description was accurate, and which other brands came up alongside it.

Paste the actual response text somewhere you control, rather than relying on memory or a screenshot you'll lose track of. A share link from the platform itself can expire or get deleted before anyone reads it again. It's not a substitute for keeping the raw text.

What a Manual Citation Check Can't Tell You

A fifteen-minute manual check like this is a real, useful signal. It is not the same thing as a full measurement, and the gap between the two is worth naming plainly.

It can't tell you whether a result is genuinely stable from only two checks. That's still a small sample of a system that changes without warning. It can't tell you why a citation is missing.

That takes checking the client's own site directly, for whether the relevant crawlers can even read it. And it can't be turned into a defensible score for a client without a documented, repeatable method behind it. A number a client could ask you to reproduce, and get a materially different answer back, is a liability in a delivery call. It's not an asset.

No specific citation, ranking, or traffic outcome is guaranteed for any brand; answer engines select their sources using undisclosed criteria that change without notice.
