How Do You Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited in AI Search?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

Sign out, open a private window, and ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google the same realistic buyer question about the client's category — not its name. Note whether the client appears, where, and how it's described. Check again a day or two later — a single check tells you what happened once, not what's true.

What's the Fastest Way to Check Whether a Client Is Being Cited?

The fastest honest check starts with a private browser window, not the one open all day.

  • Sign out of every account you can. A signed-in session carries your own history — the answer comes back shaped for you, not for a stranger.
  • Ask a category question, not a name question. Try "who handles this for a buyer like me," not "tell me about this client." Naming the brand first tells you almost nothing — most engines will happily discuss a brand you've just named, whether or not they'd surface it unprompted.

What Questions Should You Actually Ask to Check AI Visibility?

The question matters more than the platform — most manual checks get it wrong before a browser tab even opens. A single question isn't enough, because different buyer situations surface different answers from the same engine.

The four question shapes

  • Browsing. Hasn't named a vendor — "what are the options."
  • Comparing. Already knows the client's name — "how does it compare to X."
  • Constrained. A real budget, timeline, or location — "who should I actually pick."
  • Displacing. Already named a competitor — "what else is out there."

A client can look strong on one of these and invisible on another — a single "how do we show up" question only tests one of the four. A full audit runs a larger version: roughly twelve questions across these four shapes, on four platforms, producing about 48 individually tracked results.

A handful of manual checks is a sanity check, not a measurement a client can act on with confidence.

Why Does It Matter Whether You're Signed In When You Check?

Personalization is real, and it's the most common reason a quick manual check gets contradicted a week later.

  • Signed-in sessions skew results. An account with search or chat history gets an answer shaped by that history — useful to the account holder, misleading to anyone checking what a stranger sees.
  • Gemini is hard to check fully signed out. Where sign-in is unavoidable, the closest approximation is a fresh account with personalization and history-saving turned off — a known limitation, not something to ignore.

Why Check More Than Once Before Drawing a Conclusion?

These systems are not deterministic. Treating one answer as the truth is the single most common mistake in this kind of check.

  • Same question, different days, different answer. A model update, an index refresh, or ordinary variance can move the result — with nothing on the client's site changing.
  • A steady result across two days earns more confidence than one check. A result that flips between checks is itself the finding: the client's position is unsettled, not that either check was wrong.

What Should You Write Down When You Check?

Recording the answer before forming an opinion is what separates a useful check from a wasted one.

  • The question, platform, and date.
  • Whether the client was named, and where — first, buried in a list, or not at all.
  • The description's accuracy, and which other brands came up alongside it.
  • The actual response text, saved somewhere you control — not a screenshot or a share link, which can expire before anyone reads it again.

What a Manual Citation Check Can't Tell You

A fifteen-minute manual check is a real, useful signal — not a full measurement, and the gap is worth naming.

  • It can't confirm stability from two checks alone — still a small sample of a system that changes without warning.
  • It can't explain why a citation is missing — that takes checking the client's own site directly, for whether crawlers can read it.
  • It can't become a defensible score without a documented, repeatable method — a number a client could ask you to reproduce and get a different answer back is a liability in a delivery call, not an asset.
