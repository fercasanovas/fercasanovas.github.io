How Do You Build a Prompt Set to Test a Client's AI Visibility?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

Write a small set of realistic category questions, never the client's brand name. Spread them across different buyer situations: no vendor in mind, comparing named options, a real constraint, already leaning toward a competitor.

Check each on more than one platform. A handful of good questions beats dozens of near-duplicates, because the question's shape decides what it can prove.

Why Does a Question Built Around the Client's Name Tell You Almost Nothing?

Naming the brand first changes what the engine does. Most answer engines will happily discuss a company once it's named, whether or not they would have surfaced it unprompted.

A question that starts with the client's name tests recognition, not visibility. Recognition is the easier of the two, by a wide margin.

  • A brand-name question confirms the engine knows who the client is. That's a much lower bar than showing up unprompted.
  • A category question names no vendor at all. It's the only way to see whether the client enters the candidate set on its own.

What Makes a Question "Buyer-Shaped" Instead of Just a Keyword?

A buyer-shaped question sounds like something a real person would actually say out loud, with a real situation behind it. It's not a keyword a tool would generate.

It names a problem, a constraint, or a decision, in the words a buyer actually uses. Not the vocabulary the client uses internally.

  • Symptom over jargon. A buyer describes the problem. Not the category label the client uses in its own navigation menu.
  • A real constraint attached. Budget, timeline, or location changes the question. It turns browsing into something with actual stakes.
  • The buyer's own stage. Someone with no vendor in mind asks one kind of question. Someone three tabs deep, comparing options, asks another.

Why Does Coverage Across Buying Stages Matter More Than Question Count?

Ten questions testing one buying stage measure one thing, ten times. A client can look strong at the browsing stage and invisible the moment a real constraint enters. One stage never tells the whole story.

Spreading a small set across stages catches that gap. Piling on volume inside one stage does not.

The buying stages a prompt set should cover

StageWhat it actually tests
BrowsingWhether the client enters consideration when no vendor is named at all.
ComparingHow the client is characterized once a buyer already knows its name.
ConstrainedWhether the client survives contact with a real budget, timeline, or requirement.
DisplacingWhether the client surfaces unprompted when a buyer names a competitor instead.

How Many Questions Does a Real Audit Actually Run?

A full audit runs roughly twelve questions across those buying stages, on four platforms. That produces about 48 individually tracked results. Those are the same figures behind how to check whether a client is cited.

  • Twelve questions is enough to see a pattern across buying stages. It doesn't need to become a project of its own.
  • Four platforms, because a client can look strong on one and invisible on another.
  • A quick manual pass, two or three questions, is a legitimate first look. It's not a substitute for the full spread.

What Should You Avoid When Phrasing a Test Question?

The easiest way to sabotage a prompt set is writing test questions, not buyer questions. Jargon does it. So does a leading phrase, or a category label nobody outside the company uses.

The second easiest mistake is rewording one question four times and calling it coverage.

  • Leading phrases hint at the answer. Mentioning the client's category advantage inside the question tests the phrasing, not the brand.
  • Four rewordings of one question are not four questions. They test wording sensitivity, not visibility.
  • A question no real buyer would ask out loud adds noise. Even if it maps cleanly to a stage.

Where Does a Prompt Set Stop Being a Sanity Check and Become a Measurement?

A handful of questions run once is a sanity check, not yet a measurement. It's useful, but a small sample of a system that changes without warning.

  • A single run is a snapshot: it tells you what happened once.
  • A repeated run, on a schedule, turns a snapshot into a trend. That's covered in how often to re-measure AI visibility.
