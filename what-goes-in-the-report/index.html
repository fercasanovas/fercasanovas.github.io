What Should Go in an AI Visibility Audit Report?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

A complete AI visibility audit report has four parts: a scope statement, a scored result, a fix list, and the raw evidence behind each finding. Skip the evidence and the rest reads as an opinion with a number attached to it, not a finding. That's true whether the composite is computed or the report simply explains why it wasn't.

What Comes Before the Findings, in a Report That Can Be Trusted?

A report needs a scope statement before a single finding. Otherwise the reader can't judge what "no results" actually excludes.

What the scope statement should name

  • The domain and brand the audit covers.
  • The category phrase, treated as the buyer's own words, not internal jargon.
  • The platforms and the date window the questions were run in.
  • The battery size. How many questions, on how many platforms.

What Does the Scored Result Section Actually Need to Show?

The result section reports a composite when there's enough data to compute one. It says plainly why not when there isn't.

  • A computed composite stands alongside the window it was measured in. A score with no date is already stale by the time someone reads it.
  • A missing composite is a finding, not a blank page. It means the category isn't yet shaped by AI answers. That changes what gets recommended.
  • No comparison to an industry average belongs here. No benchmark dataset exists for this yet. A report that implies one is quoting a number nobody collected.

What Belongs in the Technical Findings Section?

This section reports what was actually checked on the client's own site, not a generic scan result. A technical review checks three things: whether crawlers can reach the pages, and whether the brand is described consistently. It also checks whether there's a factual footprint an engine could draw from.

  • Named findings, not a raw scan dump. Each one traceable to a specific page or specific fact, not a vague "needs improvement."
  • Pass/fail language a client can verify themselves, afterward, without taking the consultant's word for it.

What Makes a Fix List Usable Instead of Decorative?

  • Ordered by what actually blocks the result, not by what's easiest to write a paragraph about.
  • Tied to a specific finding above it in the report. Never a generic best-practice line that could apply to any client.
  • Short enough to act on. A fix list a client can't start this quarter isn't a fix list. It's a wish list.

What Should a Report Say It Does Not Claim?

A report earns trust partly by naming its own limits, out loud, before a client has to ask.

  • No traffic-attribution number. Most AI-referred visits arrive with no referrer and get logged as direct traffic. A figure here would be a guess dressed up as data.
  • No guaranteed outcome, for any platform. The client asks: can you guarantee we'll be cited? covers why that answer has to be no, every time.
  • No industry benchmark, for the reason already named above.

Where Does the Evidence Actually Belong?

The evidence sits in its own appendix, not folded into the narrative. Every entry names the exact question, the platform, the date, and the actual response. It's saved, not paraphrased, so a skeptical reader can check any finding by hand.
