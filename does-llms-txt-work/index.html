Does llms.txt Actually Do Anything?

By CassaInnova · Updated 20 September 2026

No platform has committed to reading it. So llms.txt doesn't do anything you can currently verify. It's a proposed convention, not a standard.

It isn't access control, either — a crawler that ignores it isn't breaking a rule. It costs about an hour to publish and carries no known downside. That's the honest case for having one anyway.

Is llms.txt a Real Web Standard?

llms.txt is a proposed convention. A plain-text file at the site root, describing what's on the site, meant for a language model to read instead of a full crawl.

Proposed is the operative word. No standards body has ratified it, and no major platform has published a commitment to honor it.

What llms.txt is not

  • Not a specification. No governing body. No compliance test behind it.
  • Not access control. It sits alongside robots.txt in name only. Nothing is obligated to respect it the way crawlers are expected to respect a disallow rule.

What Is llms.txt Actually Supposed to Do?

The idea is a short, curated map of a site's most important pages. And what each one covers, written for a model rather than a search index.

It's meant to save a crawler the guesswork. The same way a sitemap saves a search engine the work of discovering pages on its own.

Does Any AI Platform Currently Read It?

None of the major answer engines has published a commitment to fetch or honor llms.txt, as of today. A handful of smaller tools and crawlers do reference it. The platforms that actually decide whether a brand gets cited have not.

  • No published commitment from any of the four major answer engines to read or respect it.
  • Adoption could still arrive. The file costs nothing to have in place if it does.
  • Absence of a platform commitment is not a rumor. It's the honest, current state of the convention.

Is Publishing One Worth the Hour It Takes?

Yes, on cost alone. Writing an accurate llms.txt takes about an hour.

There's no known downside to having one. It doesn't compete with anything else on a site, and it can't break a working page.

  • Low cost, low effort. One file, written once, reviewed at the same cadence as the rest of the site.
  • No known downside. It doesn't interfere with robots.txt. It doesn't affect existing crawl access. It isn't a ranking signal to misuse.
  • Low priority, not zero. It belongs at the bottom of a fix list, after anything that actually blocks a crawler today. That's covered in how do you check whether AI crawlers can read a site.

What Does llms.txt Get Wrong When People Oversell It?

The most common mistake is treating llms.txt as access control. Something that blocks or allows a crawler, the way robots.txt does. It doesn't.

A file no platform is confirmed to read cannot be the reason a brand does or doesn't get cited. Claiming otherwise oversells a cheap, reasonable step into a fix it was never built to be.

Should You Recommend It to a Client?

Yes, described accurately. Cheap, easy, and not a fix for anything that's actually broken.

Say what it is: a low-cost, forward-looking step with no downside. Don't let it stand in for the checks that actually determine crawler access, covered in how to check whether a client is cited.

  • Say what it is. A low-cost, forward-looking step, not a fix for a real problem.
  • Don't lead a fix list with it. It's rated low-priority for a reason. Nothing measurable currently depends on it.
  • Do keep it accurate. A stale llms.txt describing pages that moved is worse than not publishing one at all.
